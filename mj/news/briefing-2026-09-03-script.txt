Good morning, Minjun. Here is your briefing for Thursday, September 3rd.

Story one. Nvidia confirmed it will acquire Hugging Face for 12.93 billion dollars. Hugging Face hosts roughly three million models, one million applications, and half a million datasets used by more than eighteen million developers. Nvidia says the platform will remain open, will continue supporting open-source and open-weight models, and will not require Nvidia compute. The strategic tension is obvious: the dominant AI chip supplier is also buying one of the ecosystem's most important model-distribution layers.

Story two. Broadcom reported third-quarter revenue of 29.6 billion dollars, up 86 percent year over year. AI semiconductor revenue reached 16.7 billion dollars, up 221 percent, driven by custom accelerators and networking. Broadcom expects that figure to rise to 21.7 billion dollars next quarter. This is another signal that the AI buildout is increasingly a networking and systems story, not only a GPU story.

Story three. OpenAI's upcoming Astra model reportedly uses a technique called recurrent depth, or opaque recurrence, that can loop through a problem outside a conventional, visible chain of thought. Safety researchers worry that scaling the technique could make model reasoning harder to monitor. OpenAI says Astra's use is limited and that preserving legible reasoning remains a core research goal. The concern is architectural and forward-looking, not proof that Astra is already unmonitorable.

Story four. A suspected breach of identity-verification provider IDScan may have exposed more than 150 million driver's licenses and passports from the United States and Canada. Security reporter Brian Krebs found his own license in the data, and the FBI says it is looking into the incident. The source company has not yet publicly confirmed the breach, so the attribution remains provisional, but the apparent dataset is real and unusually sensitive.

Story five. The United States government filed a brief supporting OpenAI's position in The New York Times copyright lawsuit. The brief argues that treating model training as outside fair use could hinder American AI leadership. It is not a court ruling, but it puts the administration's weight behind a broad interpretation of fair use for training, while leaving unresolved the separate issue of how training copies are obtained.

Story six. Amazon's Zoox expanded its paid robotaxi service to Harry Reid International Airport in Las Vegas. The company can deploy up to 2,500 vehicles under a two-year federal exemption, and its purpose-built vehicle has no steering wheel or pedals. Zoox is currently the only robotaxi operator serving the airport, but Tesla, Uber, and Waymo now also hold permits in Clark County.

Story seven. A federal judge declined to break up Google's ad-tech business despite an earlier finding that the company maintained an illegal monopoly. Google will instead be ordered to change its business practices to give competitors a fairer chance, though the detailed written remedy remains sealed for fourteen days. The decision continues a pattern of courts finding monopoly conduct while stopping short of the government's most aggressive structural remedies.

Story eight. Google agreed to buy 396 megawatts of geothermal power from Fervo Energy for a planned Utah data center beginning in 2028, with an option for another 600 megawatts. Enhanced geothermal can provide round-the-clock clean power, making it especially attractive for AI data centers that cannot rely on intermittent generation alone.

Story nine. AI-security startup HiddenLayer raised a 100 million dollar Series B after saying its annual recurring revenue grew more than tenfold in a year. Its products protect models, agents, and workflows against prompt injection, malicious tools, corrupted model files, and runtime attacks. Gartner estimates spending on AI security products will reach 2.83 billion dollars this year and nearly 4.78 billion next year.

Story ten. Microsoft is reorganizing its financial reporting into two segments called Agents and Infra, and Devices and Consumer. Beginning with its October earnings report, Microsoft will disclose Azure's quarterly revenue directly and narrow Azure to its consumption-based platform and infrastructure business. The accounting change is a useful signal: Microsoft now sees agents and infrastructure as one economic system shaped by AI.

And now, Totoro's takeaway for Minjun. Put Broadcom's numbers next to Nvidia's Hugging Face acquisition. One company is monetizing custom accelerators and the networking fabric around them; the other is buying the model-distribution layer used by millions of developers. The value chain is compressing from models, to platforms, to compute, to the links that move data between processors and sensors. For your move from post-silicon validation into automotive Ethernet digital design, that matters. AI may dominate the headlines, but reliable systems still depend on bandwidth, deterministic latency, fault containment, observability, and interfaces that behave exactly as specified under stress. Broadcom's 221 percent AI-semiconductor growth is a reminder that networking is not supporting scenery. It is part of the product. The practical career signal is to keep deepening the skills that let you prove behavior before silicon exists: protocol architecture, assertions, coverage, timing, and design-for-debug. Faster platform cycles reward engineers who can turn requirements into properties that are testable early and defensible later.

That is your briefing. Have a great day.
